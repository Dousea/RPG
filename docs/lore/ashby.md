# Ashby

## The neighbourhood

Ashby is an old neighbourhood on the east bank, a grid of brick walk-ups
between the river, the rail yards and the terminal. It grew up around the
docks and the Sallow Cannery, and most of its buildings went up in the same
twenty years to house the people who worked them: four and five storeys, iron
fire escapes, a shop on every corner. The cannery closed decades ago and is
lofts now. The docks need a tenth of the men they did.

For a long time Ashby was poor and proud of being itself. The west bank calls
it the wrong side of the river; Ashby calls itself the east bank and means
something by it. In the last few years the rents have started to climb: young
people priced out of downtown, a coffee shop with a hand-painted sign, a
gallery that lasted six months. The old residents watch this with suspicion
and some relief. The player is one of the newcomers, a few weeks in, still
unpacking.

Ashby has one clinic, one pharmacy, one supermarket and no hospital. People
who can't pay for a doctor downtown go to the Ashby Community Clinic, and that
is why the study found its volunteers here.

## The player's building

The player lives in apartment 3B of an old brick walk-up a block from the
shops: five storeys, a lift that sticks between floors, radiators that knock
all winter, a laundry room in the basement and mailboxes in the lobby. The
landlord is a company nobody has met. Marcus, the super, keeps the place
running from a basement flat beside the boiler room, and keeps the keys to
everything, including the valve for the roof tank that the building's water
comes from when the mains fail.

Emma lives next door in 3A. Down the hall, the Novaks' flat has been locked
and left since the week before the sirens. The vacant flat has stood empty
since the summer, when its last tenant stopped paying and left one night
without a word; the landlord keeps meaning to paint it.

## The shops

Each shop's state at the start of the game has a reason. Their owners shut up
and left in the days before the sirens, as the fever news got worse and the
crowds got rougher.

- **Haddad's corner store**, on the player's block. Mr. Haddad ran it for
  twenty years, open until midnight, credit for regulars. A crowd stripped
  half his shelves when the fever news broke, and he chained the front door
  from the inside and left by the back to stay with his daughter. He hasn't
  been back. The shop window is the way in now.
- **Halfmoon Coffee**, on the main street. It opened last year, the first sign
  Ashby was getting expensive. Dockworkers come in at dawn, students after the
  morning rush, and Jen knows all of them by name and order.
- **Pell's Pharmacy.** Old Mrs. Pell has filled Ashby's prescriptions for forty
  years. When a crowd came for fever medicine and the paracetamol ran out, she
  pulled the shutters down and went home to wait it out.
- **The Ashby Community Clinic**, a walk-in clinic in an old shopfront: an exam
  room, an office and a supply room. Dr. Ruth Imrie has run it for as long as
  anyone remembers. Its office has a filing cabinet with one binder in it.
- **The FreshWay**, Ashby's supermarket. It locked its doors after shoppers
  fought over bottled water; the staff went out through the loading bay and
  the manager took the keys.
- **Dunmore Hardware**, in the same family since the docks were busy. Old Walt
  Dunmore locked up and drove upriver to his son's before the sirens.

## Places around the shops

- **Tanner Park**, a strip of grass and plane trees with a bandstand, where
  Ashby walks its dogs and drinks on summer nights. The evacuation buses loaded
  here for two days. The last ones left half empty, honking for anyone still
  coming.
- **Ashby station**, the Blue Line's stop under the main street. The trains
  stopped the morning of the sirens. The gates are pulled shut, and something
  on the other side rattles them.
- **Dock Road**, the main road down to the terminal, lined with chip shops,
  bookmakers and a police station. Its shop windows went in on the night the
  evacuation was announced.
